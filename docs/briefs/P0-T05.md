# P0-T05 Spike B: VRM avatar and lip sync — brief

Owner: main (architect-direct). Depends: P0-T01, P0-T02b. ADRs: 02, 03, 11.
Surface facts: `docs/SURFACE.md`, "Avatar stack for P0-T05", 2026-09-09.

## The question

ADR-03 puts a full-body semi-realistic VRM 1.0 avatar in three.js behind an `AvatarRenderer`
interface, on the assumption that a browser can drive one at a conversational frame rate while the
voice pipeline is also running. Nothing has tested that. Phase 2 builds the stage on top of it and
Phase 7 builds a character pipeline feeding it, so if the frame rate is not there — or if three-vrm
and react-three-fiber cannot be made to agree with React 19 — both phases change shape.

Three things this answers:

1. **Does it render, and how fast?** A VRM 1.0 with MToon materials, spring bones and node
   constraints, in react-three-fiber, on a discrete GPU and on integrated graphics.
2. **Does the mouth work?** `wawa-lipsync` from an `<audio>` element to the five VRM mouth
   expressions the protocol defines.
3. **Does the animation path exist?** A `.vrma` clip loaded through `@pixiv/three-vrm-animation`
   and applied to the humanoid.

## What is already known, and must not be re-derived

Read `docs/SURFACE.md` before writing a line. The three findings that shape the work:

- **`wawa-lipsync` speaks Oculus, not VRM.** Fifteen visemes (`viseme_PP`, `viseme_aa`, `viseme_E`…)
  against VRM's five plus silence. **Nine of the fifteen have no VRM equivalent.** The mapping is
  ours to write, ours to test, and the place this spike is most likely to produce something that
  technically runs and looks wrong.
- **`three` is pinned to 0.185.1**, not the latest. 0.186.0 is inside pnpm 11's release-age window,
  and three-vrm is developed against `^0.180.0`.
- **The sample avatar is redistributable but not CC0 or CC-BY.** ADR-11 wants CC for assets, so it
  is fetched at run time behind a consent screen rather than committed. Same for the animation.

## The mapping is the real work

Nine Oculus visemes have no VRM mouth shape. Guessing is what makes an avatar look like it is
chewing. Decide it explicitly, write the reason next to each line, and test it:

- The five vowels map straight through: `aa→aa`, `E→ee`, `I→ih`, `O→oh`, `U→ou`.
- `sil→sil`.
- The consonants are approximations to the nearest **mouth shape**, not the nearest sound. A closed
  bilabial (`PP`) is nearer to silence than to a vowel; a wide fricative (`SS`, `CH`) is nearer to
  `ih` than to `aa`. Write down which and why.

Weight comes from `lipsync.features.volume`, since the library exposes a winning viseme rather than
per-viseme weights as a property. Clamp it, smooth it, and say what the smoothing constant is — an
unsmoothed mouth snapping between shapes at 60 fps is the other way this looks wrong.

## Measuring frame rate honestly

Per frame, from `requestAnimationFrame` deltas:

- **Median and 5th-percentile fps** over a fixed window, not the instantaneous number. A mean fps
  hides exactly the stutter a person notices.
- **Worst frame time** in the window, called out separately. One 80 ms frame is visible; it does not
  move a median.
- Warm-up frames discarded, and how many, stated.

Report at a fixed canvas size — the fps of a 400 px viewport says nothing about a 1080p stage — and
record the size, the device pixel ratio, and the adapter the browser actually gave.

**Integrated and discrete both.** WebGL takes a `powerPreference` hint, so a `low-power` context is
the cheap way to reach the integrated adapter on a machine that has one. If the browser ignores the
hint and reports the same adapter twice, **say so and report one number**, rather than printing the
discrete figure under two headings — the Spike A rule.

## Consent comes first (ADR-09, and ADR-11 here too)

The avatar is 10.8 MB and the clip is 11.5 KB, both from GitHub. The screen lists size, licence and
source, and names the licence honestly: the VRM Public License 1.0, **not** Creative Commons, which
is why it is not in the repository. Nothing is fetched before the button.

## Tests

`visemes.test.ts` — this is where a silent defect becomes a chewing avatar:

1. Every one of the fifteen Oculus visemes maps to a member of `VisemeSchema`. Exhaustive over the
   enum, so a library update that adds a sixteenth fails the test rather than falling through to a
   default.
2. The five vowels map to their own VRM shape, not to a neighbour.
3. Weight is clamped to `[0, 1]` — `volume` is not bounded by the library.
4. Smoothing moves toward the target and never overshoots it, and a silence target decays to zero
   rather than sticking.

`frame-rate.test.ts`:

5. Median and 5th percentile over known deltas, including the even-count case.
6. Warm-up frames are excluded, and a window shorter than the warm-up reports "not measured" rather
   than a number from two samples.
7. Worst frame time is the worst frame, not the worst average.

The r3f scene itself is not unit-tested — it needs a GPU, and that is what the run is for.

## Notes for whoever reads this later

- The sample VRM is a *constraint twist sample*, not a character. It is the right thing for
  measuring and the wrong thing for judging how the product looks. Do not let a screenshot of it
  become the reference for what Alice should be.
- `test.vrma` has three animation channels. It proves the loader and nothing else; say that in the
  write-up rather than calling it an idle.
- Spring bones and MToon are the expensive parts of a VRM, and both are on in this file. That makes
  the measurement pessimistic in a useful way — a simpler character will be faster, not slower.
